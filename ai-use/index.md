Editorial Standards

# Statement of Principles on Artificial Intelligence

How RDP uses AI and computational tools while keeping journalism human-led, verified, transparent, and accountable

Effective Date: August 9, 2026  ·  Version 3  ·  Last Updated: August 22, 2026

Assistance isn’t authority.

Journalists are still responsible for what we write. AI doesn’t decide what we believe, it doesn’t choose what we publish, and it doesn’t absolve us of the obligation to verify our reporting. The final work is human-directed, human-edited, human-approved, and human-accountable.

At a glance

-   Our reporting is human-led. AI can assist our work; it does not decide what we report.
-   We verify consequential factual claims against original records or reliable sources.
-   AI-generated output is not treated as evidence by itself.
-   We protect confidential sources and sensitive information.
-   We do not use AI to fabricate reporting, impersonate sources, or mislead readers.
-   Humans retain editorial responsibility, including responsibility for corrections.

## Human-Led, Original Reporting

Every investigation published by Restoring Democracy’s Promise is conceived, directed, verified, edited, and approved by a human journalist. AI-assisted tools may contribute to parts of the reporting and publishing process, including research organization, document analysis, outlining, language suggestions, coding, visualization, or editing. But the investigation itself—its questions, evidence, conclusions, sourcing, framing, and final publication decision—is our original work.

We do not publish autonomous AI journalism. We publish human journalism that may use modern computational tools.

AI assistance does not transfer authorship or accountability away from the journalist.

## AI Is a Tool

Restoring Democracy’s Promise shares many people’s concerns about the expansion of data centers and their consequences, deepfakes, misinformation, and the spread of a technology that can be used to manipulate, surveil, exploit, or diminish human agency. That’s why we think it’s more important than ever before to use the same technology for the opposite goal: to uplift humanity through finding and disclosing the truth.

At the same time, we realize that artificial intelligence is being increasingly integrated into _existing systems of power._ Governments, corporations, political parties, law-enforcement agencies, data brokers and other powerful institutions use automated systems to surveil populations, identify targets and opportunities, make predictions, communicate at scale, manage information, shape decision-making, and hold enormous amounts of data about our lives.

Artificial intelligence itself is a tool. Like any powerful technology, its value to democracy depends on who wields it, how they’re using it, how transparent that use is, and who suffers when it fails.

Those tools aren’t going away.

So the question journalists and citizens should be asking isn’t whether we can go back to a world without these tools. The question is whether immense analytical power will remain concentrated in institutions’ hands — or whether journalists, citizens, researchers, and public-interest organizations will also be able to harness modern technology to understand, analyze, and investigate those institutions and the systems of power within which they operate.

Our view is straightforward:

The public shouldn’t be expected to face those institutions armed with nothing more than pen and paper while the institutions themselves become increasingly technologically empowered.

## Why We Use It

Restoring Democracy’s Promise uses AI-assisted tools because modern journalism often requires analyzing institutions that operate on volumes of information that can be impractical for a single reporter to analyze efficiently without computational assistance. Those powerful institutions are increasingly integrating AI into their own systems.

Thousands of pages of contracts. Massive databases. Court filings. Years of procurement records. Legislative histories. Lines of technical code. Mountains of financial data. Layers of data-sharing agreements. Pages of public-record requests. Complex flows of money between agencies and contractors.

AI can help us sort that information, identify connections worth investigating, compare one document to another, find contradictions, summarize technical material, assist with data analysis, build visual explanations, and proofread our writing for clarity and errors.

As an engineering-informed investigative publication with a growing [analytics practice](https://restoring-democracy.org/analytics/), we believe public-interest journalism should be capable of using advanced computational and analytical tools to scrutinize governments, corporations, political networks, surveillance systems, and other powerful actors that already employ those capabilities themselves.

## Computationally Assisted Counterpower

Our stance is one of computationally assisted counterpower.

Institutions will continue to harness computational power. Journalism should have the right tools to investigate those institutions.

We don’t believe the answer to centralized technological power is for everyone else to blindly disarm ourselves of technology.

The answer is transparency, access, accountability, verification, and democratic control of technology.

## Assistance Is Not Authority

But assistance isn’t authority.

AI doesn’t get to decide what RDP believes. It doesn’t choose what we publish. And it doesn’t absolve us of our obligation to verify our reporting.

Journalists are still responsible for what we write.

We choose the questions we ask.

We fight to obtain the records we need to do our jobs.

We evaluate the information we uncover.

We test conclusions against the evidence.

We differentiate documented fact from inference.

We judge what is ethical to publish.

We correct our work if we’re given evidence that we’re wrong.

The final work is human-directed, human-edited, human-approved, and human-accountable.

That matters.

There is a massive moral difference between feeding raw data into an algorithm that decides whether someone should be surveilled, investigated, fired, disenfranchised, or labeled suspicious — and using technological tools to analyze how those determinations are made.

Morality isn’t baked into technology.

Powerful entities and their purposes always exist behind the technology. Transparency, accountability, and consequences always matter.

AI detection is not truth detection.

A detector score cannot establish whether reporting is accurate, original, properly sourced, plagiarized, or false. These systems make probabilistic judgments about patterns in language, and those judgments are imperfect. RDP evaluates journalism by its evidence, sourcing, attribution, provenance, reasoning, and willingness to correct errors—not by a meter’s guess about how a sentence may have been produced.

We judge originality through provenance, attribution and evidence—not probabilistic guesses about whether prose “sounds AI-generated.” Plagiarism is an attribution and provenance problem, not an AI-detection problem. We take plagiarism seriously regardless of whether AI was involved.

AI tools may help develop an outline, suggest language, organize research, compare documents, or produce an initial block of text. But no AI output is published automatically. Every article is reviewed, edited, fact-checked against the available evidence, and approved by a human. The human author decides what survives, what changes, what is removed, and what is ultimately published.

Our work may be AI-assisted. Editorial responsibility is human.

## Evidence Over Detection

We reject two naive arguments.

We do not believe every use of AI should be accepted at face value simply because it’s powerful or new.

But we also will not marginalize, ridicule, or distrust any journalistic work merely for being “AI-assisted.”

AI tools can help flag claims, inconsistencies, and patterns for verification, but humans are perfectly capable of assessing evidence without probabilistic sentence-level detectors policing what we write.

A 100% human-written article can be entirely false.

An AI-assisted article can be exhaustively sourced and completely accurate.

Strong evidence should be the basis for our judgment.

Does the reporting answer questions about its evidence?

Can the claim be supported by documents or firsthand sources?

Are the documents provided genuine?

Does the author represent sources fairly?

Does the reporting disclose uncertainties?

Can another reporter read the documentation and reach their own conclusion?

Will the reporter or publication correct significant errors when presented with evidence they are wrong?

Does the byline represent a human who stands behind the work?

Those are far more useful questions than trusting an algorithm to attempt to guess how a piece was written, assign a score, or make a moral judgment based on that score alone with no assessment of truth.

Nothing about AI should obscure that journalism.

Our obligation is to present readers with the best-supported understanding the evidence permits. Technology does not change that obligation. It is part of the DNA of Restoring Democracy’s Promise.

## The Danger of Automated Judgment

We are especially concerned about automated systems that reduce complicated human decisions to a simplistic score or category.

Citizens become risk scores.

Voters become “database matches.”

Neighborhoods become crime predictions.

Researchers become an “AI percentage.”

Investigative journalism becomes slop.

It’s dangerous when society starts treating these quantifications as more than what they are: proxies for complicated human realities.

Sure, a classifier can spit out a statistical probability that indicates a pattern it has been trained to identify. But that doesn’t mean the algorithm understands the person, the research process, the evidence, or the truth of the underlying work itself.

When scores start becoming credentials — as opposed to tools to be examined — people start optimizing themselves to please the metric.

Journalists start writing _to please detectors instead of to inform readers._

Lawmakers start treating neighbors as data points to be governed rather than constituents to be represented.

Slowly, human judgment is eroded in favor of whatever can conveniently be measured by a computer program.

Journalism should be investigating that future, not accepting it tacitly.

**We strive to publish the best-supported understanding the evidence permits, regardless of what an AI-detection meter says about the prose.**

## Permitted Uses & Prohibited Uses

Permitted uses may include:

-   organizing and searching large document collections;
-   comparing records and identifying potential contradictions;
-   summarizing technical or legal material for further human review;
-   assisting with data analysis, coding, calculations, and visualization;
-   developing research questions and investigative leads;
-   suggesting outlines, headlines, explanatory language, or draft passages;
-   editing for clarity, grammar, structure, and accessibility;
-   testing our own reasoning by generating competing explanations or identifying weaknesses in an argument.

But AI output is never evidence simply because a model produced it.

We do not permit an AI system to independently determine what is true, whom to accuse, what evidence is sufficient, or what should be published.

We do not publish fabricated quotations, invented sources, nonexistent documents, synthetic factual claims, or citations that have not been independently verified.

AI may suggest. Humans decide.

## Sources, Privacy & Sensitive Information

Not all information should be treated alike simply because a technological system can process it.

Public records, court filings, published research, legislation, contracts, and other openly available materials may be appropriate for computational analysis.

Confidential sources, unpublished tips, sensitive personal information, legally protected records, and material whose disclosure could place someone at risk require greater care.

RDP evaluates whether information should be submitted to an external AI system based on its sensitivity, the purpose of the analysis, the privacy implications, and the protections available.

AI convenience does not override source protection.

Sensitive material is not submitted to third-party systems unless we determine that the available privacy, retention, and security protections are appropriate for the material involved.

We will never knowingly expose confidential source identities or sensitive unpublished material merely because doing so would make analysis easier.

## Authorship, Synthetic Media & Disclosure

AI-assisted systems may help organize research, analyze records, suggest language, generate an outline, assist with visualization, or produce draft material for human revision. That assistance does not transfer authorship or editorial responsibility to the machine.

No AI-generated text is published automatically.

The human author determines what is retained, rewritten, rejected, verified, contextualized, and ultimately published.

Where synthetic or AI-generated imagery is used as illustration rather than documentary evidence, we will clearly identify it whenever a reasonable reader could mistake it for a real photograph, event, person, document, or piece of evidence.

We will not use synthetic media to fabricate evidence or deceptively portray a real person as having said or done something they did not say or do.

When AI plays a materially significant role in an investigation or presentation, we will disclose that role in a way that gives readers a meaningful understanding of how the technology was used.

Our standard is not disclosure theater. It is meaningful transparency.

## Costs, Limitations & Continuous Review

AI systems are imperfect.

They hallucinate. They can reproduce bias. They can misunderstand context. They may generate confident answers unsupported by evidence. Different systems can produce different answers to the same question.

They also have physical costs.

Large-scale computation requires energy, water, hardware, data centers, and infrastructure. We share legitimate concerns about the environmental and community impacts associated with the rapid expansion of those systems.

We do not believe those costs disappear merely because AI is useful.

Our responsibility is therefore twofold: use computational tools where they materially improve public-interest journalism, while remaining conscious of their technical, social, environmental, and democratic costs.

We will favor purposeful use over novelty for novelty’s sake.

And because these technologies are changing rapidly, this statement is not intended to be frozen in time.

RDP will revisit these principles as the technology evolves, as journalism develops better standards, and as evidence changes our understanding of the benefits and harms involved.

## Our Commitment

Where those tools improve our ability to gather information on matters of public consequence, we’ll continue to use them.

-   We’ll be transparent about how we use them.
-   We’ll remain skeptics of those tools.
-   We’ll test their conclusions.
-   We’ll disclose their limits.
-   We will never treat what a machine tells us as evidence unless it is independently verified against the underlying records, data, sources, or other evidence.
-   And we will remain accountable for our work.

We don’t expect readers to trust our work just because we’re human. And you shouldn’t automatically distrust us because we work with machines and computational analytics.

You should expect better than that from anyone claiming to speak truth to power.

You should hold us to the same standards you hold governments, corporations, banks, and algorithms.

Trust us in proportion to the evidence we provide.
