Statement of Principles on Artificial Intelligence

Why we’re “AI-assisted” and don’t have a machine certify our work

Effective Date: August 9, 2026  ·  Last Updated: August 9, 2026

Assistance isn’t authority.

Journalists are still responsible for what we write. AI doesn’t decide what we believe, it doesn’t choose what we publish, and it doesn’t absolve us of the obligation to verify our reporting. A human being signs our work and accepts responsibility for it.

AI Is a Tool

Restoring Democracy’s Promise is neither pro-AI nor anti-AI.

We are AI-agnostic.

Artificial intelligence is a tool. Like any powerful technology, its value to democracy depends on who wields it, how they’re using it, how transparent that use is, and who suffers when it fails.

Governments, corporations, political parties, law-enforcement agencies, data brokers and other powerful institutions use automated systems to surveil populations, identify targets and opportunities, make predictions, mass communicate, manage information, shape decision-making, and hold enormous amounts of data about our lives.

Those tools aren’t going away.

So the question journalists and citizens should be asking isn’t whether we can go back to a world without these tools. The question is whether immense analytical power will remain concentrated in institutions’ hands — or whether journalists, citizens, researchers, and public-interest organizations will also be able to harness modern technology to understand, analyze, and investigate the powerful entities those institutions serve.

Our view is straightforward:

The public shouldn’t be expected to face those institutions armed with nothing more than pen and paper while the institutions themselves become increasingly technologically empowered.

Why We Use It

Restoring Democracy’s Promise uses AI-assisted tools because modern journalism often requires analyzing institutions that operate on volumes of data no human can comprehend unassisted.

Thousands of pages of contracts. Massive databases. Court filings. Years of procurement records. Legislative histories. Lines of technical code. Mountains of financial data. Layers of data-sharing agreements. Pages of public-record requests. Complex flows of money between agencies and contractors.

AI can help us sort that information, identify connections worth investigating, compare one document to another, find contradictions, summarize technical material, assist with data analysis, build visual explanations, and proof our writing for clarity.

Assistance Is Not Authority

But assistance isn’t authority.

AI doesn’t get to decide what RDP believes. It doesn’t choose what we publish. And it doesn’t absolve us of our obligation to verify our reporting.

Journalists are still responsible for what we write.

We choose the questions we ask.

We fight to obtain the records we need to do our jobs.

We evaluate the information we uncover.

We test conclusions against the evidence.

We differentiate documented fact from inference.

We judge what is ethical to publish.

We correct our work if we’re given evidence that we’re wrong.

And yes, ultimately a human being signs our work and accepts responsibility for it.

That matters.

There is a massive moral difference between feeding raw data into an algorithm that decides whether someone should be surveilled, investigated, fired, disenfranchised, or labeled suspicious — and using technological tools to analyze how those determinations are made.

Morality isn’t baked into technology.

Powerful entities and their purposes always exist behind the technology. Transparency, accountability, and consequences always matter.

Evidence Over Detection

We reject two naive arguments.

We do not believe every use of AI should be accepted at face value simply because it’s powerful or new.

But we also will not marginalize, ridicule, or distrust any journalistic work merely for being “AI-assisted.”

AI tools can help flag claims, inconsistencies, and patterns for verification, but humans are perfectly capable of assessing evidence without probabilistic sentence-level detectors policing what we write.

A 100% human-written article can be entirely false.

An AI-assisted article can be exhaustively sourced and completely accurate.

Strong evidence should be the basis for our judgment.

Does the reporting answer questions about its evidence?

Can the claim be supported by documents or firsthand sources?

Are the documents provided genuine?

Does the author represent sources fairly?

Does the reporting disclose uncertainties?

Can another reporter read the documentation and reach their own conclusion?

Will the reporter or publication correct significant errors when presented with evidence they are wrong?

Does the byline represent a human who stands behind the work?

Those are far more useful questions than trusting an algorithm to attempt to guess how a piece was written.

Nothing about AI should obscure that journalism.

The Danger of Automated Judgment

We are especially concerned about automated systems that reduce complicated human decisions to a simplistic score or category.

Citizens become risk scores.

Voters become “database matches.”

Neighborhoods become crime predictions.

Researchers become an “AI percentage.”

Investigative journalism becomes slop.

It’s dangerous when society starts treating these quantifications as more than what they are: proxies for complicated human realities.

Sure, a classifier can spit out a statistical probability that indicates a pattern it has been trained to identify. But that doesn’t mean the algorithm understands the person, the research process, the evidence, or the truth of the underlying work itself.

When scores start becoming credentials — as opposed to tools to be examined — people start optimizing themselves to please the metric.

Journalists start writing to please detectors instead of readers.

Lawmakers start treating neighbors as data points to be governed rather than constituents to be represented.

Slowly, human judgment is eroded in favor of whatever can conveniently be measured by a computer program.

Journalism should be investigating that future, not accepting it tacitly.

Computationally Assisted Counterpower

Our stance is one of computationally assisted counterpower.

Institutions will continue to harness computational power. Journalism should have the right tools to investigate those institutions.

We don’t believe the answer to centralized technological power is for everyone else to blindly disarm ourselves of technology.

The answer is transparency, access, accountability, verification, and democratic control of technology.

Our Commitment

Where those tools improve our ability to gather information on matters of public consequence, we’ll continue to use them.

We’ll be transparent about how we use them.

We’ll remain skeptics of those tools.

We’ll test their conclusions.

We’ll disclose their limits.

We will never accept what a machine tells us as evidence until we verify it ourselves.

And we will remain accountable for our work.

We don’t expect readers to trust our work just because we’re human.

And you shouldn’t automatically distrust us because we work with machines.

You should expect better than that from anyone claiming to speak truth to power.

You should hold us to the same standards you hold governments, corporations, banks, and algorithms.

Trust us in proportion to the evidence we provide.
