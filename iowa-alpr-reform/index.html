Iowa's ALPR "Reform" Bill Would Regulate Plate Readers — and Hide the Logs

A bill sold as oversight requires the records that would reveal misuse — then quietly classifies those same records as confidential under Iowa Code 22.7.

Session update As of the close of Iowa’s 2026 legislative session, SF 2284’s ALPR language did not receive final agreement and the bill did not pass. That is a good outcome for public accountability: the proposal added some guardrails, but it also would have shielded key usage records from public view. Core Thesis
The bill requires the government to create the accountability record — then prevents the public from seeing it.

Iowa lawmakers promised to "rein in" license-plate readers. What the final package may deliver instead is a statewide ALPR system with official blessing, expanded data-sharing channels with law enforcement and insurance companies — and a statutory clause that locks the only logs that could prove misuse from public view.

That is why this bill has to be read in two layers: a visible reform layer that reassures the public, and an infrastructure layer that protects the system.

Interactive — The Two Layers

The bill operates on two simultaneous layers. Toggle between them to see what's visible to the public — and what isn't.

Provision
Local Ordinance Requirement
Cities must pass an ordinance before deploying ALPRs, naming approved vendors and the job classifications authorized to run searches.
Provision
Facial Recognition Ban
All ALPR systems must operate without facial-recognition capability. Limited in practice — ALPRs are plate-recognition systems, not facial ones. The real fight is over location data and logs.
Provision
30-Day Raw Data Deletion
Plate images and associated data must be purged after 30 days — unless copied into a separate case file. The exception matters.
Provision
Mandatory Audit Trails
Each ALPR search must log officer name, call number, and reason for search. Agencies audit logs every four months — but the public will never see those audit results.
Hidden Mechanism
Statewide Normalization Engine
Requiring ordinances and a DPS vendor list doesn't limit ALPRs — it standardizes them. A patchwork of private contracts becomes a state-sanctioned network, politically easier to expand.
Hidden Mechanism
Broad Data-Sharing Channels
The bill authorizes sharing plate data with any "peace officer or law-enforcement agency" (including federal), contractors on public-safety tasks, and — explicitly — insurance companies and the National Insurance Crime Bureau.
Hidden Mechanism — H-8452
Iowa Code 22.7 Secrecy Clause
A single sentence inserted at end of session: "A search log maintained pursuant to this subsection is a confidential record under section 22.7." Agencies can deny public-records requests by default. Audits still happen — internally. The public never sees the results.
Hidden Mechanism
Vendor Access Exceptions
The bill bars agencies from conveying historical location data to vendors — except when anonymized, when needed for operation, security, testing, or improvement, or for technical support. Those exceptions cover most normal vendor operations.

Evidence Cards — Key Mechanisms

Tap any card to expand the full mechanism.

01
Mechanism — Secrecy Clause
The Single Sentence That Locks the Logs
▾

Amendment H-8452 added one sentence to the logging section of SF 2284:

⚠ Statutory Text — H-8452
"A search log maintained pursuant to this subsection is a confidential record under section 22.7."

Iowa Code 22.7 is the state's enumerated list of records agencies "shall keep confidential" — covering medical records and investigative materials. Once placed there, public-records requests can be denied by default. Before this bill, agencies could still fight over ALPR logs case by case. H-8452 hands them a statutory shield to refuse disclosure altogether.

02
Mechanism — Data Sharing
Insurance Companies Get Plate Data. Permanently.
▾

The House amendment explicitly authorizes sharing plate data with insurance carriers, the National Insurance Crime Bureau, and "insurance support organizations" for fraud investigations, vehicle recovery, or claims adjudication.

There are no clear deletion or minimization requirements attached to this sharing. Commercial industries gain access to government-collected surveillance data with limited strings attached.

Municipalities are licensing Iowans' data to private, third-party vendors that are sharing it around the world. Iowans are losing control over their data in ways that are unnecessary to advancing public safety goals.
Becca Eastwood — ACLU of Iowa Policy Director

03
Mechanism — Normalization
A Patchwork Becomes a State-Sanctioned Network
▾

Requiring local ordinances and a DPS vendor list doesn't limit ALPRs — it standardizes them. Before SF 2284, police could deploy Flock Safety or similar systems with minimal public input and hold data indefinitely.

After SF 2284, those same systems get official state blessing through the DPS approval process. Two-year vendor cycles and approved lists make it politically and practically easier to scale up, not down. The patchwork becomes infrastructure.

Source: SF 2284 / S-5192
DPS Vendor Framework
04
Mechanism — Vendor Exceptions
The "No Vendor Access" Rule That Permits Vendor Access
▾

The bill bars agencies from conveying "historical location information" to a plate-reader vendor — except when:

  • The data have been anonymized or "redacted"
  • The vendor needs historical data for operation, security, testing, or improvement
  • The vendor provides technical support

Those three exceptions describe routine vendor operations. The rule as written permits the access it purports to restrict.

Mechanism — Process
End-of-Session Darkness: When Bills Get Their Worst Amendments
▾

The secrecy amendment emerged during the final days of Iowa's 2026 session — as lawmakers debated bills past midnight. Both chambers recessed, reconvened, and stood at ease repeatedly in the early hours.

It was in this window that H-8452 was filed. A 16-item amendment that — among tweaks to mobile unit caps and signage — classified search logs as confidential under Iowa law. Major surveillance architecture decisions made when public attention is lowest.

Key Takeaways
The visible layer is not the full system. Reform provisions are real but structurally constrained by exceptions that preserve the surveillance architecture.
The only accountability records are sealed by law. H-8452 classifies search logs as confidential under Iowa Code 22.7, turning internal audits into a closed loop the public cannot enter.
Insurance companies are an explicit beneficiary. The bill names insurance carriers and the NICB as authorized recipients of ALPR data — with no clear deletion requirements.
The patchwork becomes permanent infrastructure. State-level vendor approval doesn't limit the system — it gives it official legitimacy and a scalable framework to grow.
The fix is available: remove the 22.7 confidentiality sentence, restore online audit publishing, narrow sharing definitions, add warrant requirements for out-of-state and federal sharing. None of those things are currently in the bill.
A privacy bill that doesn't address private vendors isn't a privacy bill. It is a half measure.
Rep. Ryan Weldon (R-Ankeny) — Iowa House
