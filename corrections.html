Corrections & Updates

How we fix mistakes, update reporting, and revise data — transparently

Effective Date: February 10, 2026  ·  Last Updated: February 10, 2026

No Stealth Edits. No Quiet Rewrites.

If we publish something wrong, we correct it — plainly, visibly, and with a timestamp. If we learn something new, we update the story and explain what changed. In a world built on manufactured confusion, transparency isn’t a vibe. It’s a requirement.

1. Purpose

Restoring Democracy's Promise (RDP) exists to publish accountability journalism that can withstand scrutiny. That means we treat errors as a credibility emergency — not a public-relations problem. When we are wrong, we correct the record as soon as possible and as clearly as necessary.

2. Corrections vs. Clarifications vs. Updates

We use plain labels. We do not hide behind euphemisms.

CORRECTION (factual error)

A correction is used when we publish a factual mistake (for example, a wrong name, date, quote, number, location, or mischaracterization of a document). Corrections are labeled as Correction and include what was wrong and what is correct.

CLARIFICATION (meaning was unclear)

A clarification is used when the original wording could reasonably be misunderstood, even if strictly factual. Clarifications explain the intended meaning without changing the underlying facts.

UPDATE (new reporting)

An update is used when new information becomes available after publication (for example, a new filing, a new response from an agency, or a material development). Updates add context or new facts and include a timestamp.

Small fixes (typos, formatting)

Minor edits that do not change meaning (such as spelling, grammar, or formatting) may be fixed without a published note. If a change affects meaning, it gets an editor’s note.

3. How to Report an Error

If you believe we made an error, please contact us and include enough detail to verify the issue quickly.

  • Subject line: “Correction Request”
  • Include: the URL, the specific passage, what you believe is wrong, and supporting evidence (documents, links, screenshots, or citations)
  • Optional: your name and how to reach you (anonymous requests are accepted, but harder to verify)

4. What Happens After You Contact Us

  • Acknowledgment: We aim to acknowledge correction requests promptly (typically within a few business days).
  • Verification: We review the claim against primary documentation wherever possible.
  • Action: If an error is confirmed, we correct it with a clear note and timestamp. If we disagree, we will explain why.
  • Accountability: When appropriate, we describe how the error occurred and what we changed to prevent repeats.

We apply the same standard to text, images, charts, timelines, datasets, code, and interactive exhibits.

5. Where Corrections Appear

  • On the article: Corrections and substantial updates appear on the story page, typically near the top or bottom (or both), labeled clearly.
  • On related assets: If the error affects a chart, dataset, infographic, or embedded exhibit, we update that asset and note the revision.
  • In newsletters: If a significant error was distributed via email, we may publish a correction in a subsequent newsletter issue.

6. Data, Charts, Graphics, and Code

RDP publishes “systems journalism,” which often includes structured datasets, document collections, and engineered visualizations. We treat those as publishable claims — and therefore correctable.

  • Data revisions: If a dataset changes materially, we will describe what changed (sources, filters, deduplication, definitions, fields, or methodology).
  • Versioning mindset: Where feasible, we keep a dated revision history so readers can understand the evolution of the work.
  • Methodology notes: If a correction changes a conclusion, we will say so directly.

7. Social Media & Syndication

If we share a story on social media and later issue a significant correction, we may:

  • post a follow-up noting the correction,
  • update the original post where the platform allows, and/or
  • link directly to the corrected article.

If an error appears in republication or quoting by others, we may contact the downstream publisher where feasible.

8. Unpublishing / Takedown Requests

We do not remove published work simply because someone dislikes accurate reporting. We consider unpublishing only in rare circumstances, such as:

  • credible risk of imminent physical harm,
  • court orders we cannot legally challenge,
  • demonstrably false material that cannot be responsibly corrected, or
  • serious legal/ethical failures that require retraction.

If unpublishing occurs, we aim to leave a transparent record (for example, a retraction notice explaining what happened and why).
